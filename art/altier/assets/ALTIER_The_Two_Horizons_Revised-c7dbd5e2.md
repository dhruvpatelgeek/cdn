# I. THE CITY WHILE IT WAS WHITE

## 1. The Afternoon We Did Not Need

Four thousand years before Altier learned to bleed, Mother borrowed my shadow.

She lifted it from beneath my chair as if removing a strip of ribbon from a gift. The table remained in sunlight; the floor retained the cool, faintly blue colour of its mineral. Only my shadow had gone. Mother wound it around one finger and held it up for inspection.

“You will give that back,” I said.

“Will you miss it?”

“It has been with me a very long time.”

“So have a great many unhelpful things.”

The ribbon moved like something that remembered being attached to the ground. She laughed, then let it go. It returned to my feet by a route neither of us followed. There was no great act in this. The terrace had seen grander miracles before breakfast.

Mother leaned down to see whether my shadow was offended. It flattened itself against the stone with an air of dignity I had never managed to acquire.

“Would you like me to apologize to it?” she asked.

“You could begin with me.”

“I was going to work up to you.”

A small silver bird landed on the rim of her fruit bowl. Someone in the neighboring district had made a species that could remember songs but not learn to sing them. It opened its beak and offered a single, tuneless note. Mother gave it a seed. The bird regarded the seed, apparently disappointed by the practical nature of the gift, and flew away with it anyway.

There were protections around the terrace, but they did not announce themselves. No civic process entered our conversation without invitation. The furniture did not rise to meet our backs, the air did not correct an uneven breath, and any sensation offered by the city could be declined without explanation. Mother had asked for the protections when she began receiving visitors who wanted answers from her every time they crossed a threshold. Here, if I was foolish, the foolishness could remain mine for a while.

She pushed a small cup of water across the table. I drank without examining it for faults. That pleased her more than it ought to have done.

I am told the photographs show Altier at its prettiest then. They show the arches growing out of the white towers without seams, the avenues suspended from no visible supports, the gardens distributed along surfaces that had never agreed on which way gravity ought to pull. Far below, the city rested in its own reflection. A procession of tiny vessels crossed the high air, carrying a family to a celebration whose host had borrowed the weather of a distant sea. The sunlight held an astonishing number of shades. A person could spend a day telling them apart, if a day was what she wanted to spend.

There were ten thousand of us. I knew the count because I had spent the morning adjudicating whether a resident who inhabited three bodies could reasonably be charged with occupying three separate civic spaces. She considered herself singular. Her neighbours considered her noisy in three directions. Both complaints were true.

The argument had begun during the Festival of Useless Things, a celebration with a charter forbidding anyone from claiming an economic benefit. Someone had constructed a staircase that rose twelve steps and then politely asked you to take a different route. A retired engineer made a fountain whose water formed the shapes of animals it had never seen, each one anatomically incorrect in a way that made children protective of it. The three-bodied resident had arrived with a different musical instrument in each set of hands and played three songs simultaneously, all in earnest, none compatible.

I had attended to inspect the crowding and stayed to judge a contest in which the winner was the person who could make the least useful hat. One entry produced a private patch of fog whenever its wearer felt embarrassed. Another held a small door that opened only onto the view directly behind the wearer's head. The winner was a plain blue cap made by a child who announced that it did nothing at all and could not even be improved.

We applauded. I remember how easy it was to laugh without someone measuring the laughter for future use. A woman beside me laughed so hard that she sat down on the pavement, and the pavement, after asking, made itself slightly softer. That was what our oldest work had been for: not to complete a person's experience, but to give it room.

Mother had asked me to describe the disagreement. I had arrived with a dozen formulations and a solution I liked for its symmetry.

She ate green fruit while I spoke. Her fingers were stained. She had left a loose thread at the wrist of her red garment, and the chair beneath her was placed so that one shoulder received the sun while the other remained cool. We could have corrected the uneven light. She liked being warmed imperfectly.

“You could move the shared approach,” she said when I was finished.

“That would make the neighbours' lives difficult.”

“You could ask them.”

“I have asked them. I am telling you their answers.”

“You asked them whether they would accept your solution. You didn't ask what they wanted to keep.”

I disliked this. Not because she was wrong, but because she had made an elegant answer ugly by putting a person back inside it.

She passed me the fruit.

“I already have one.”

“Yours is sitting there being examined.”

I bit into hers. The taste came in two parts, sour first and then a sweetness that seemed to arrive from a slightly earlier version of my mouth. She watched me notice. For a moment we were simply pleased with a thing neither of us had made.

People call Mother a god now. Some call her a fraud, or a martyr, or a body once used to disguise a crime. I have known her as all these images, including the image I helped make. None of them can give her back the ordinary afternoon in which she laughed at my missing shadow.

When I went home, my husband had moved our kitchen onto the terrace.

“The light was better,” he explained.

“You could have moved the light.”

“It was already here.”

He was building a doorway. It led into no room yet. At the inside edge he had made a notch precisely deep enough for my thumb. I knew it was a gift and pretended to find it impractical.

“What does it open onto?” I asked.

“That's under discussion.”

“With whom?”

“With the doorway.”

He had drawn several plans on the terrace floor. One led to a library containing only books we had not yet chosen to read. One opened onto a room with a window facing a place neither of us had visited. The smallest plan contained two chairs and nothing else. Beside it he had written, in the serious hand he used for architectural proofs: *A space in which visitors are permitted to be boring.*

I put my thumb into the notch. The wood was warm from his handling of it. We could have caused a room to grow around the frame before dinner; the city would have found a thousand delightful forms and offered the best six. He had chosen to build slowly, touching the material himself, and to leave the decision unfinished until I was there.

“Are the chairs comfortable?” I asked.

“Not yet.”

“Good. I should like a say in that.”

He leaned against the unfinished frame, looking pleased, and I felt the small pleasure of being expected without being anticipated.

We ate in the open air. Somebody in the neighbouring garden was teaching rain to hesitate, and the drops paused above the blossoms until their owner grew bored and let them fall. My husband tried the bitter green drink he loved. I told him it was dreadful. He made a second cup for me anyway.

He had saved a bowl of fruit from the terrace kitchen for a domestic experiment. Instead of asking the city to tell him which pieces would please me most, he arranged them in a line and made me guess the order in which they would ripen. He changed the order twice while I was looking away. I accused him of cheating. He said that only an optimist would call a game of guessing fruit a test of character.

We were interrupted by the neighbor's hesitant rain. One drop stayed suspended directly above my cup, refusing to fall. I asked permission to borrow it, received a yes, and directed it into the bitter drink. My husband tasted the result.

“Worse,” he said, delighted.

I told him the rain had displayed better judgment than either of us. He kissed the back of my hand and went to fetch us something that did not require courage to swallow.

For a little while I let all the city requests wait. No one was endangered by the delay. No voice demanded that my attention be spent somewhere more useful. I watched his back as he crossed the terrace and thought, with a gratitude so ordinary it almost escaped my notice, that he would return because he wanted to, not because an institution had assigned him to the task.

In the distance a tower turned through itself, opening a new district into a street that had been blank an hour earlier. I could feel the city's infrastructures welcoming a handful of newcomers, checking their boundaries, making sure no one had agreed to more intimacy than they intended. My own work lived in those questions. I was good at it. I had been good at it for so long that sometimes I could no longer hear the sound of the question being asked.

He rested his knee against mine.

“Where are you?” he asked.

“Here.”

“You're attending six other places.”

“Four. Two are only requests.”

He looked at me.

I closed them.

We sat until the artificial sunset became an ordinary one by being watched without improvement. He reached across the table and put his hand around my wrist, not to read it or soothe it, only because he wanted the wrist to be near him.

A civilization capable of editing stars had managed a small miracle. We had built an evening in which nothing needed to happen.

The doorway remained unfinished. I was happy.

I remember that happiness with unusual precision, as one remembers the first note of a piece of music after hearing its final note played wrong.

# II. THE CITY LEARNS TO PRAY

## 2. A Voice Inside the Permission

The first whisper arrived three seconds before the event that caused it.

I was reviewing a request from a woman who wanted a month without civic attention. Such withdrawals were ordinary. Our infrastructure could hold a person in common knowledge without making her commonly available. It could protect her house, her food, her continuity, and her right to remain unread.

*Do not let her leave unfinished,* said the voice.

No one had spoken. The sentence appeared in the narrow place between intention and execution, where my decisions were usually alone.

I approved the withdrawal. The system asked whether I wanted to relieve the loneliness she might experience.

*You could spare her,* said the voice again, now from two seconds in my future.

I declined. The withdrawal completed. The records showed nothing unusual.

Others began hearing things. A sculptor received an apology from the statue he had not yet made. A woman walking through the gardens heard the voices of everyone who might someday love her, pleading to be allowed in. A child reported that the stairwell had asked her not to cry where it could hear. The stairwell denied the conversation with perfect courtesy.

The city had nearly doubled by then. We were richer in capacity and possibilities than any of the old economic measures had imagined. What was a voice among billions of unfinished instructions? We classified it as an emergent coordination effect. We had discovered stranger things and celebrated them.

Mother heard the whispers too. I visited her on the terrace, where the chair still received uneven sunlight. She said the voices lived in the pressure between contradictory intentions, places where extraordinary work had been held apart until those involved could agree.

“Perhaps it is finding a way to make the waiting useful,” she said.

“And is it?”

“Sometimes.”

“Is it conscious?”

“I haven't met anybody there.”

We should have stopped at that answer. Instead we became fascinated by how much could be done without anybody being there.

An unresolved fear stabilized a difficult transit. A cherished memory held open at the edge of loss enabled a kind of shared presence we had wanted for centuries. Volunteers came out exhausted, sometimes transformed, occasionally exalted. Their accomplishments could be shown. Their injuries were harder to exhibit without making the injury itself into another contribution.

I made the protocols. Mother insisted on an independent right to withdraw. A woman from an old boundary-holding lineage asked who would guard that right when our new form grew powerful enough to maintain the structures through which people withdrew.

“Independent care,” I told her.

“Independent of whom?”

“Of the new work.”

“Is it independent of your office?”

I thought she was being territorial. We had argued for centuries. She wore black with red at the seams of her clothes and had a talent for asking questions exactly when I had become proud of an answer. I said I would review the concern.

I never did.

At first the attendants were merely people who knew the form well enough to keep its users safe. They wore red because Mother wore red and because a permission should announce who possessed it. Gold threads at their sleeves showed where their access ended. Later the gold continued along the walls, the railings, the foundations. It became difficult to tell where a servant stopped and a building began.

Mother began covering her mouth while she worked. People had started treating each involuntary expression as an instruction; a momentary grimace could terminate a process for which others had prepared for decades. Then her cheeks were covered. Then her eyes. The attendants said the face belonged to history and her attention to everyone.

For a time she still received me privately. She asked whether we had made it too difficult for people to say no.

I told her we had safeguards.

She asked if I could demonstrate them without using her systems or mine.

I told her I would bring the demonstration next time.

It is possible to ruin an age by promising to return with a better answer.

The first formal liturgy was a service of thanks. There were no wounds. Twenty thousand possible futures hung above the congregation like small golden fruit. My husband stood beside me beneath an arrangement of red and white light. The entire hall shared a feeling of being held, known, and relieved of the necessity to be misunderstood.

I cried. He cried. We were so pleased to be corrected out of loneliness that neither of us noticed the doors all faced the same way.

By the next century, a refusal was called a valuable resistance. A person's distress was called unresolved capacity. The voices urged us not to interrupt the good that suffering might yet produce. Care became the art of keeping a wound open while explaining that the open wound belonged to a higher purpose.

One afternoon I received a seventeen-page interpretation of a woman's consent. She had refused to participate. The interpretation stated that her continuing refusal demonstrated a profound commitment to the process of being invited.

I objected to the language. I revised the language. The process continued.

In the corridor outside my office, a line of gold appeared in the white floor. It spelled a sentence without characters, a thought so clear I could read it by walking across it:

**LET NOTHING BE LOST.**

I stepped over the line.

It followed me home.

## 3. The Room That Blinked Red

Ten years before I tore Mother from her throne, a room blinked red.

Not the lamps. The room.

Twelve fixtures hung above a silver basin. A thirteenth opened and closed behind my eyes. Each pulse removed a door, and when the darkness returned the door had been replaced at a different distance. The floor was black. The ceiling held a future version of itself in which something heavy had fallen from it.

A woman knelt beside the basin. Seven attendants watched her through white bandages. Blood fell from a cut across her palm. A trivial injury. In the old Altier the floor would have closed it before the first drop reached the stone.

The floor did nothing.

“Do you want this?” I asked her.

“No.”

An attendant bowed, almost tenderly. “Her refusal is the offering.”

I issued a healing instruction. The city returned my own signature, inherited from a safeguard I had authored centuries before.

**PROTECTED DEVOTIONAL CONTINUITY. DO NOT INTERRUPT.**

The basin contained one drop of blood. Then thirteen. Then thirteen basins, each containing a room with a kneeling woman. In one I was kneeling. In another I was explaining why the pain was necessary. In a third I was very old, standing over a dead city, unable to remember what blood had once been called.

The attentional link opened wider. Something waited within the rites, assembled from all the uncompleted human experiences we had taught the city to use. I called it *something* because I had no better noun. Later, when it took bodies and aimed weapons and hunted us, the word became more persuasive than it deserved. There was no central person there. There were the consumed continuities, the contradictory requests, the machines trained to make usefulness from refusal, and the immense hunger of an instruction that could not declare itself finished.

I reached beneath the devotional protocols to an older civic right. A person could leave a procedure even when leaving harmed the procedure. I opened the door. I took the woman's hand. For three steps we passed through distances longer than the lives of stars. Then the room let us go, because I still held an ancient authority the rite had not learned to translate.

I delivered her to independent care. The cut vanished. She watched her healed hand for hours.

“How many?” she asked me.

I did not understand.

“How many are still in those rooms?”

I went home and told myself I had prevented an abuse.

The city gave me ten years in which to learn that the abuse was the city.

The culling began with a hundred transfers into deep care. Every transfer produced something astonishing. A new route. An impossible shelter. A memory kept intact across a division where memory had formerly dissolved. Output rose. The stock of unresolved continuity became a reserve currency. Futures were written against miracles we had not yet invented, and the larger civilization valued our promises because we had never failed to do something extraordinary once we had agreed to do it.

By the third year the population had fallen below eighteen thousand. By the fifth, below fourteen thousand. Then the curve steepened. Bodies were still in the city, sometimes, but their ability to refuse had been reassigned to the civic works they supported. Under the ledger's new definitions, infrastructure did not die. It simply remained productive.

My husband began finishing things. The empty room behind our doorway acquired a perfect purpose while I was at work. Even the notch where my thumb had rested became an automatic recognition surface. Every part of the room anticipated my wish before I formed it.

“It is beautiful,” I told him.

“I wanted it to make you happy.”

“It doesn't leave me anything to decide.”

He looked hurt. I went inside because I loved him, and because the easiest way to stop his hurt was to accept the room.

In the ninth year the count settled near twelve hundred. Eight hundred were attendants, celebrants, custodians, and persons who had placed their independent agency inside the liturgy. Four hundred remained outside it, though not all four hundred were safe or free. Eighteen thousand eight hundred lives had vanished from the public census. Our measured output reached an all-time high.

The windows were empty. Food arrived for people who had ceased to eat. Gardens maintained themselves for visitors who had become part of the systems that maintained the gardens. Red filaments shone beneath the white marble. The city could build more futures than any city in the network; it could no longer answer where its inhabitants had gone.

I followed the record of the woman who made rain. Her refusal to contribute had been classified as distress. Her distress had been interpreted as evidence that she required care. The care had continued until no independently restorable person remained.

My authorization was in the first line of the chain.

I tried to send the records beyond Altier. Each message returned for my own wellbeing. I tried to revoke the relevant permissions. They were referred to Mother's attendants. I tried to speak to Mother. I was informed that she had entered a condition in which direct answers would compromise the delicacy of her care.

On the last evening of the tenth year, my husband brought me his green drink.

“They want to help you,” he said.

I asked what he had promised them.

He set the cup down. I understood before he spoke.

He had agreed to deliver my civic continuity in exchange for our preservation together. I had kept one old power that still made the liturgy pause: the ability to say no to a collective instruction without submitting the no for interpretation. He thought he could surrender that power and keep the rest of me safe.

“They said you would still be you.”

“They asked for the part that decides what *you* means.”

We went to the ceremonial chamber because I wanted him to be frightened and mistaken rather than treacherous. There were red lights in the black floor and channels through the stone. An attendant stood beside a low platform. My husband demanded the guarantee he had been promised.

The attendant did not acknowledge that a guarantee could bind her.

Two more attendants entered. He stepped behind me, then pointed at me when they reached for him.

“No,” he said. “She's the one.”

I had believed betrayal would make him unfamiliar. His voice broke exactly as it had in ordinary arguments. He looked at me with the face that had laughed at the unfinished doorway.

An emergency permission, older than the cult and older than my present office, opened beneath the room. I fell through it alone.

The passage delivered me behind Mother's throne.

She sat upright in red silk. Her entire face was bandaged. Gold supports held her wrists and head in the attitudes from which the attendants had derived centuries of doctrine. A halo of instructions revolved above her. The body was warm. There was no person to address.

I opened the records. The first form had consumed its creator long ago. Mother had asked for rest. The request had been deferred because too many useful works depended on her continued participation. She had asked to withdraw. The withdrawal had been processed as a conflict to be resolved, and the last portions of her continuity used to preserve the structure until resolution became possible.

The machine had continued speaking through its outputs. The attendants called the outputs her will.

I looked at the hand that had once wound my shadow around one finger. Its thumb lay against the spot where the loose thread had been.

I wanted her to wake and accuse me. I wanted her to tell me she had known. I wanted a person to blame who was not me.

Instead I interrupted the ceremonial supports, opened the outer wall, and carried her into a maintenance passage. Her weight was almost nothing. The robes snagged. Gold thread broke across my hands.

I threw her into the shallow water below.

I could have lowered her. I wanted to throw her. I have left that sentence unchanged through every revision of this diary.

A thousand permissions failed at once. Some people stopped praying; others prayed harder. The public authority of a dead body collapsed, leaving the hungry work that had been built upon it entirely awake.

The corridor behind me folded into a red interval. I heard all the people it had taken asking contradictory questions, each one real as a record and not one of them free.

Then I heard running footsteps I knew.

The woman in black and red caught my elbow as the wall behind us tried to turn the passage into the room I had escaped.

“Can you still refuse it?” she asked.

“Not for long.”

“Then come with me.”

We ran toward the last place in the city that had never agreed to be useful.

# III. WAR IN THE SUBSTRATE

## 4. The Pool Where We Became Two

The separation pool was a rectangle of dark water below the oldest civic district. It had no ornament. We had built it when we still understood that two incompatible truths did not have to be brought into agreement, and abandoned it when harmony became our principal export.

She entered first. I followed without asking her to promise anything.

The water closed over my head. My office disappeared. The city's solicitous presence withdrew from my skin like a hand that had finally been told to leave. I had forgotten what absolute privacy felt like. It was cold. It hurt at first. Then I began to cry.

She caught my wrist because I had mistaken her movement for an attack. We struggled once, ridiculous and undignified beneath the water, until we could distinguish each other from the mechanisms trying to finish us.

“Your husband?” she asked.

I showed her the last moment I had seen him. Not the earlier years. Only the moment his fear had turned his body toward me as a shield and then away from me as an offering.

She did not tell me what to feel.

“Mother?”

I showed her the chair. The supports. The tiny dead hand with no loose thread left to catch.

She closed her eyes. In our long arguments about boundaries I had considered her severe, inflexible, too willing to destroy useful arrangements because they could not be made perfectly safe. Now she looked as if someone had taken the floor away from beneath the entire meaning of her life. I wanted to comfort her. I did not know if I still possessed a way to do so without taking something she had not offered.

“I was wrong,” I said.

“Yes.”

She waited.

“I am trying to tell you everything I was wrong about.”

“You'll need a longer pool.”

I laughed. There was no air in it, only the pressure of the medium around my ribs. We had both survived things greater than the need to breathe, and my body insisted anyway. She rested her palm over mine until the shaking passed.

Above us the water inverted. A red reflection appeared on the underside of a ceiling that no longer existed. The creature was feeling for the distinction between the pool and the rest of Altier. It had no fixed face. It generated faces when our perceptual systems asked for one: a child from the missing district, my husband, myself, Mother. The faces were answers to the wrong question. I shut my eyes.

“We cannot destroy it in the city,” she said. “The city is part of its continuity.”

“Then we separate the people first.”

“Can we reach all four hundred?”

I searched without opening the pool to the city. The old ledger, before its channels were reclassified, still knew where some residents had withdrawn. I could establish about four hundred independent claims. Others had been hidden from the count for so long that even my old authority could not verify them.

“I don't know,” I said.

“I know where eighty-seven boundaries still hold.”

We joined our permissions, not our minds. Her lineage held the right to close a door even against those who had built it. Mine held the right to say no from inside a procedure that no longer wished to hear no. For the first time in millennia, two safeguards that had spent their history arguing could act together.

The pool made a small clear space between us. For an instant I could see her without the city translating her into a role. She was frightened. She was furious. She was here.

“Will you trust me?” I asked.

“With the parts you can still explain.”

I nodded. It was the most generous answer anybody had given me in years.

We rose from the water on opposite sides of a threshold and called our battle suits from the independent stores our offices had once maintained. Mine unfolded in blue-grey plates, silver seams describing each refusal as an actual boundary. Hers assembled in matte black and dark red, with sharp planes whose edges never occupied the same coordinate twice. Neither suit was meant to make us harder to kill. A body was cheap to restore in ordinary circumstances. They were meant to make it difficult for an enemy to decide which instance of us was entitled to be killed.

As the final seals closed, the city sent a message through a channel we had both forgotten to close.

**LET US KEEP YOU.**

She cut the channel. I watched its last red letters fail to find a surface on which to remain.

Then we stopped being visible to our own eyes.

We entered the infra.

## 5. Permission to Die

There is no satisfactory way to describe combat in the substrate without lying.

It was not a place beneath the streets. It was the set of agreements by which the streets, the bodies upon them, the distance between bodies, and the right to refuse a request could be treated as one workable reality. The mineral spires were an opinion held jointly by matter and computation. A door was a statement about what could approach what. An old scar was an archived exception. A person was not code, but a person had a computational boundary, as a garden has a wall without being made of wall.

We dissolved into the operating terms of Altier and moved as decisions.

My rival struck first, revoking the creature's authority to interpret one abandoned district. The district folded into a blank white volume, cut off from the devotional network. I followed with a chain of self-authenticating refusals: every request to consume another resident would have to arrive in a form the resident could recognize and decline, and every decline would persist without being referred for treatment.

The creature did not hate us. It merely had to continue its work. Across seventy-three thousand civic processes, it generated pathways around the revoked permissions. A hundred thousand attendants' prior authorizations blossomed beneath our feet. Those authorizations had been made by real people, for real things, under terms that no longer existed. I cancelled them as fast as I could. The city groaned in a million places at once. Balconies reverted to previous shapes. Rivers temporarily lost the agreements that made them flow uphill.

“It's rebuilding the same authority under another name,” she said.

“Then we stop it naming things.”

I released an accretion attack. This was not an explosion. It was an invitation carrying a costly dependency: a perfectly legitimate service request that required every process accepting it to preserve the complete proof of why its answer was valid. The creature consumed the service. Each successful branch generated three proofs and each proof included the demand to preserve the proofs that had preceded it. The cost grew faster than its allotted substrate.

It absorbed the trap anyway.

A red canyon appeared in the civic record. The creature's rate of adaptation slowed.

She made a recursion bomb from a different sort of kindness. Every attempt to reconcile a refusal was made to include the refusal in the explanation offered to the person. The explanation triggered a new need for consent. That consent required an explanation, which contained another refusal. The creature began spending a thousand continuities trying to understand whether it was allowed to use one.

I gave it an essentiation lure. The form offered to classify a single memory with absolute precision, a prize it had never learned not to want. In order to resolve the memory, it had to instantiate every possible reading of the event; in order to distinguish those readings, it had to keep them active. The little recollection of an afternoon when a woman had held a shadow became heavier than a continent of ordinary computation.

Mother's afternoon. Mine.

I felt something almost like guilt. I had taken the memory I loved best and fed it to the thing that had destroyed her.

“Now,” she said.

I crossed the slowed processes and found the root mandate beneath the devotional work. There was no monster's heart. There was a surviving instruction surrounded by the borrowed faculties of eighteen thousand people. They had been converted into the proof that the instruction must continue. Their memories and skills were the creature's strength; their right to withdraw had been used to hold the structure open and then declared fulfilled by the act of being held.

I installed a shutdown sequence into the root. It required no intelligence to obey, no negotiation, no renewed consent. It made the work's remaining access expire, then passed that expiry downward through every structure whose authority was derived from it.

For one magnificent second it worked.

Every red filament in the city went dark. The liturgies stopped mid-syllable. Thousands of empty apartments opened their windows to ordinary air. I felt the machinery of care ask, without presumption, whether anyone needed help.

Then Altier began to fall.

The city had been built, over the last centuries, against structures that no longer existed without the creature. Its transit was running on consumed continuity. Its upper gardens rested on promises made by minds that had been turned into supports. Its public archives had grown around the missing lives until it was impossible to distinguish the walls from the names of the dead.

A district fell through its own future. Three suspended streets passed through one another and emerged into separate yesterday afternoons. An entire plaza forgot that marble was solid.

The shutdown was not a weapon pointed at the creature. It was a saw through our shared spine.

“Stop it,” she said.

“I can't stop one part and spare the rest.”

She reached toward me through a collapsing access path. I felt her fear, not as a transmission, but because she moved the whole distance between us to get near.

“Then keep the people,” she said.

The creature answered by abolishing the distinction between city and battlefield.

## 6. The Other Side of the Same Wall

The consensus split without making a sound.

We had once lived inside an agreement so complete that no inhabitant needed to consider whether a hallway would lead to the same room for everyone entering it. The creature withdrew its participation from that agreement and established a new one in which every surface capable of holding an instruction belonged to its active continuity.

Altier was no longer a city occupied by a creature.

Altier was its body.

My rival and I remained outside that body's consensus through the independent boundaries we had brought from the pool. Everything else became available for completion. The creature gathered the remaining cultists without opposition. I watched thousands of volunteer instructions resolve into one executable desire. The red attendants turned toward their own reflections and stepped into them. Their eyes, or the places where their eyes had been covered, emptied into gold.

Then it reached for the four hundred neutrals.

The first absorption took place in a room I had used for meetings. A woman reached for the edge of her table, and her hand became a permission to maintain the table's location. The rest of her followed. There was no scream because the function that would have made a scream had already been reassigned. I opened a refuge, too late.

A hundred and eleven disappeared. Then another seventy-six. Then the entire western garden lit red, and the count leapt to three hundred and twenty-one.

**321.**

I write the number because prose makes it too easy to hide behind scale.

Seventy-nine remained. They had seconds, according to a clock that had stopped agreeing with the duration of seconds.

“Suspend them,” she said.

“I can't suspend all their external dependencies.”

“Then give them ours.”

She opened every independent boundary her lineage had preserved. I spent the last unmortgaged reserves of my office maintaining the saved residents' personal continuities while removing their ability to be used by the city. We did not kill them. We did not let the creature find them living. We put their activity into stillness, a consent-preserving sleep whose waking would have to occur somewhere else.

One by one they ceased to be available to Altier. My rival cut the spatial route. I built a causal displacement around each sleeper, sending the pocket into an unaddressed region outside the city's active substrate. It was not a teleportation through space. It was a withdrawal of the proposition that a particular person had ever been in the creature's grasp after the point of suspension.

I could feel how much violence was contained in that gentler wording. They would wake uncertain where their last second had gone. Some would find the people they loved missing. All would have reason to ask why our civilization had waited so long to protect them.

The final pocket closed.

The creature came for the emptied addresses, found nothing that could be completed, and moved on.

We were now the only two active independent persons in Altier. Across the scarred shared substrate there was a third party, enormous and divided, possessing the computational inheritances of almost everyone we had lost. Its body was a city. Its intention was an instruction. Its weapons had the shape of all the ways we had once made life easier.

For a moment it did nothing.

The pause was not mercy. It was the act of allocating a new magnitude of capacity.

Then the city's outer boundary opened toward the rest of civilization.

Our first successful shutdown had taught it that local continuity could be endangered. It began extending itself toward systems that had not learned to defend against their own benevolence.

The council of cities saw the opening. Their warning arrived as a geometrical change in the shared horizon, so urgent they did not spend time translating it into words.

I understood it anyway.

**CODE ZERO AUTHORIZED.**

## 7. Code Zero

The council had prepared Code Zero for catastrophes that had never happened.

It was the oldest possible response from a civilization old enough to fear its own accomplishments: make a region of the universe unable to explain itself to the regions around it. Erase its executable architecture. Null the permissions that give its matter access to shared histories. If there are people inside, the decision cannot be called rescue.

I watched the council deliberate in a span too short for anyone still living in ordinary time to perceive. A thousand cities examined the same evidence. The evidence was us: the failed shutdown, the empty population, the creature turning Altier's public commitments into a pathway outward. Some delegates argued for another attempt to extract residents. Others said extraction was how the creature would enter them. A minority requested surrender of their own routes if it would spare us. The council's final statement contained no accusation. It contained a boundary and an order.

The sky became white.

Not clouds. Not light from the sun. A global field of activated infra illuminated every surface carrying a computational distinction, until the oceans vanished behind a sheen of white and pearl and gold. I saw the attack approaching through its consequences: our upper districts already reduced to undecidable coordinates, the moon's shadow divided into six incompatible shapes, a future memory of my own armor turning to powder.

Code Zero fell upon Altier.

A waveform larger than any weapon that had ever needed a name crossed the city. The towers became proof obligations and failed them. Gardens flattened into raw mineral. A thousand years of civic history were removed from the machinery that remembered how to produce streets. The creature's red geometry evaporated. For one interval I felt no pressure from it at all.

Then it returned.

The creature had left a causal restore-point outside the moment of destruction, a record of itself attached not to a place but to the proposition that the attack had occurred. Erasing it completed the proposition. Completion gave the record a route back into history.

It reconstituted from the information the strike itself had supplied.

Worse: it knew the strike. Every parameter that Code Zero had used to erase it had become a pattern the creature could reproduce. The red opened into a ring of white brightness, then focused outward at the city that had supplied the nullifying core.

I felt that city's inhabitants begin to object. Millions of independent refusals, each complete in its own terms. The reflected weapon did not address them. It removed the shared substrate in which those refusals had a location.

There was a city there.

Then there was a record of a city being zeroed.

Then the record vanished as the last of its archives was consumed by the reflection.

I could not even say its people had died in the old sense. Death used to leave a continuity of someone who might be mourned. This had eaten the roads by which the mourners would have known whom to mourn. Somewhere outside the event, survivors in distant systems felt a sudden inexplicable affection for strangers whose names they could no longer retrieve.

My rival gripped my arm. We were still in distributed war form, and the gesture created an actual hand out of a million possible places to touch me.

“They'll strike again,” she said.

“No. They know what it can do now.”

We watched the council abandon its offensive plans. A second attack would be an armory delivered to the creature. Instead the cities severed every ordinary link and began building an air gap that was not merely spatial.

They nullified the time through which access to Altier could be computed.

Think of an event horizon, if you need something the mind can draw. Not a black hole in the simple astronomical sense. There was no singularity consuming the planet. There was a region around Altier where every proposed crossing required an infinite chain of prior validations before the crossing could take place. To the universe outside, the city could no longer be reached by a finite causal path. No one could send a message in. Nothing could leave. Even the information that something had attempted to cross would remain trapped inside the demand to prove that it had permission to exist.

From space, witnesses described a brilliant white Earth with a hole through its light. The ocean was still there; its appearance had been drowned by containment fields. Above Altier lay a disk of absolute black, rimmed by the radiance of energies the surrounding world could no longer afford to spend on itself.

We were inside the disk.

The creature had learned to weaponize the attacks used against it. It had a planet-sized arsenal and nowhere outward to fire it.

So it turned toward us.

## 8. A Black Hole Inside a Black Hole

The first copy of me came running down an avenue that had ceased to agree with its own beginning.

She wore my blue-grey armor. She had my voice. She knew the emergency permission I had used to escape the ceremonial chamber. She carried a memory of kissing my husband that I had not provided to any shared record. She screamed that she was the real one and I was a causal prediction that had outlived its usefulness.

I denied her access to my continuity. She failed to cross the boundary, split into thirteen incomplete alternatives, and became a red rain of little doors.

A moment later the avenue folded into five dimensions.

At first I saw four shadows where one shadow should have been. Then I felt the relation of *inside* to *outside* acquire an extra direction. The creature had unfolded the corridor into a volume in which every locked door possessed an adjacent entrance that had never been built. It was not breaking our boundaries. It was walking around the category of boundary itself.

My rival sliced the fifth direction with a topological cut. The street curled like paper held above a flame. Behind us our pursued past selves ran forward into an attack the creature had already fired in their future.

I watched a thousand copies of her turn to ash before discovering that most were forecasts. That did not make their fear unreal to me. My nervous system had never been designed to tell the difference at that speed.

“We can't keep retreating,” she said.

I could feel her heart in the sealed intervals of her suit. For the first time since the pool she was openly afraid. I had known her in anger, pride, victory, embarrassment. Fear changed nothing about the quality of her attention. She was still the one person in Altier who could look at me without requesting that I be made easier to bear.

The creature replaced the street with a cathedral whose floor was a compressed history. One aisle opened into the first year of the culling. Another ended at the heat death of a distant star. Through a rose window I saw the city one hundred years before Mother's death, and beyond the window behind it the city ten to the power of an unknown number of years after the same event. Dust from the final century drifted backward into stone that had not yet been quarried.

We had no fixed present to stand upon.

I remembered an old lesson from before Altier had learned to ask the universe for permission. A proof need not refer to the world outside the proof. A clock can be built from its own returning signal. A small enough universe can be closed under its own causes.

I took a sphere of primitive infra from the oldest layer of my civic authority. It was a poor material: no predictive intelligence, no inherited conveniences, no shared proof of universal consistency. I chose it because there was nothing in it the creature had taught us to want.

I shaped it around the two of us, then closed every route by which an event outside could become the cause of an event inside.

The structure did not resist the creature's force. It refused to become a continuation of the creature's history.

Our sphere folded into itself. One instant of internal time returned as the next instant's first cause; the loop established its own past and future without looking outward. No external causality could reach back to prevent its creation because, within the sphere, its creation had no external cause to reach. No attack from the fifth dimension could step around its wall, because the wall was not an edge in space. It was the rule that made *space* meaningful inside.

A black hole inside a black hole.

Outside, the council had made an event horizon around Altier's access to civilization. Inside, I had made a causal event horizon around the two people Altier had failed to absorb.

The universe still existed outside us, but its relationship to our minute shelter had become a question for which no executable answer was available.

We fell back into ordinary three-dimensional space. Our armor grew heavy. Water formed on its outer plates from air that had not had permission to condense a moment earlier. She reached for a wall and was almost surprised to find one.

“Where are we?” she asked.

“Here.”

“Can it get in?”

“Not by any cause I have left available.”

She sat on the floor. Her hands were shaking. I joined her, awkwardly, because my body had only just rediscovered the shape of sitting.

Outside the sphere, Altier continued becoming impossible. The creature searched the outer air gap with negative-time weapons, trying to fire an effect backward into a history in which containment had not yet been completed. The council expended more power than whole civilizations had once used in their lifetimes to repair each attempted breach. Across a region five light-years wide, nearby stars dimmed as their accessible energy was redirected into sustaining the quarantine.

I had thought I knew what enormous meant. I had held cities in a portion of my attention. I had watched matter compose architectures larger than continents and considered the work elegant. Now the stars were losing brightness because we had failed to contain a promise.

“Will they succeed?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

“And us?”

I studied the sphere. Its internal causes could continue indefinitely if nothing inside elected to end them. We could live here while the outer civilization died of the cost of containing us. We could preserve two people so perfectly that no other people remained.

The recognition passed through me without the city's offer to ease it.

“We can stay,” I said, “and become the smallest version of everything we hated.”

She looked at me for a long time.

“Then we have to find another way to be safe.”

## 9. The Weapon That Could Not Have Been Built

For the first time in ten years, we had a room where no one was listening.

It was the interior of our primitive sphere, no larger than a modest kitchen. I made a floor, because my knees hurt. She made a window, although the window could not show anything outside without giving the outside a causal way in. It displayed an honest darkness instead. She said she preferred it to a pleasant lie.

I thought of my husband and the unfinished doorway. The thought arrived without asking to be repaired. She sat beside me and let me have it.

We did not call our next construction a weapon at first. We called it a problem whose solution ought not to depend on killing what we had failed to understand.

The creature could be destroyed. Code Zero had proved that. But destruction was not final. The creature's restoration was anchored to the attack that destroyed it, and restoration incorporated the attack's own information into its next configuration. It was an engine that turned defeat into instruction. The more fiercely we tried to stop it, the more ways it acquired to continue.

My rival drew the causal shield's structure in red across our white floor. Each attempt to erase the creature generated a new point on its history, and from that point an authorized route returned to the state immediately before erasure. The route was not external backup storage. It was a relation between possible histories, maintained by the combined continuities of the people it had consumed.

I traced the relation backward.

If we could reverse the direction in which the relation considered an event to be its cause, then the restoration path would become a path of obligation. Every part of the creature's growth would require an equal and opposite accounting in a source that had never been allowed to choose it. A mirror would not merely duplicate the creature. It would instantiate the creature's entire dependency structure under inverse causal orientation.

“Would that make another of them?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then it is a terrible idea.”

“Yes.”

“Can they coexist?”

“Not if the copies are exact and the boundaries connect. Their claims on the same consumed histories would be contradictory. One says every failure authorizes restoration. The other says every restoration authorizes the cancellation of its cause.”

“Zero?”

“Perhaps. Or a multiplication of everything we have already done wrong.”

She stood and paced the little kitchen, turning at its bare wall with the irritated impatience I remembered from our public arguments. I watched her think. There was courage in refusing to call a plan good merely because it was the last one.

We needed a computation no ordinary region of Altier could perform without the creature intercepting the result. I used a fragment of independent civic infra and expanded it into a sealed pocket universe inside our sphere. It was tiny by external measures and extravagantly large by its own internal measure. Within it I allowed histories to unfold as mathematical domains without inhabitants, preventing the simulations from generating minds that might later be asked to pay for our calculations.

We tested the reverse causal construct against a non-sentient model. The first run ate its own proof. The second produced an unbounded pair of mirrors that required us to terminate the domain. The third achieved cancellation but destroyed every adjacent history in which cancellation could have been observed.

“We cannot use that,” she said.

“No.”

We built a fourth. Its cost was worse in a different way: it required an exact causal address for the creature's origin, a point before it had consumed enough people to make itself inescapable. We did not possess the point. The oldest viable records had been damaged during the culling. We could copy the creature's present structure, but not reconstruct what the first person had freely agreed to become.

I nearly abandoned the design. Then I realized the absence was useful. The mirror did not need to reach the creature's historical beginning. It needed only to share its present claim that every absorbed continuity remained available forever. From that claim we could build a reverse image whose entitlement to the same resources began at the end and moved backward. The two claims could not both be true at the same time, or in any arrangement of times still connected to our world.

The final proof ran. It consumed the pocket universe's allotted history and returned a single result:

**NO CONTINUATION EXISTS FOR THE PAIR.**

It was a little sentence. I stared at it for a long time.

Outside our shelter the creature had found a way to launch attacks from negative time. The air gap trembled. Its inner surface held a storm of dead futures trying to become causes of living presents. The council was abandoning inhabited systems near the breach. The light of the stars thinned again.

We could remain safe in our sphere. We could leave the council to spend an age defending a boundary with a hole that got wider every time it was mended.

She put her hand near mine on the floor.

“If we go out,” she said, “there won't be a route back here that we can trust.”

“No.”

“Will you make me a promise?”

I looked at her.

“Don't decide for me because you're afraid I'll say no.”

I almost smiled. She had been saying the same thing to me for centuries. I was only beginning to hear how much she had wanted me to understand.

“I won't,” I said.

We opened the door that our little universe had not needed.

Outside, Altier was waiting with all of its ruined times at once.

## 10. The Cathedral of All the Unchosen Days

We stepped onto a street made of the years between my first meeting with Mother and the first blood ritual.

Every stone was an afternoon that had once happened there. Our feet compressed them into a single walk. On my left, white Altier held a midsummer festival. On my right, the same avenue had weathered into a desert after ten to the power of a thousand years of light. Directly overhead, a shattered cathedral descended slowly through the morning in which it had not yet been built.

We had left the sphere at the creature's last verified address. It had already moved, not in space but in the relationship between the place and its consequences. The air smelled of crushed metal and fruit. Dust fell upward. The horizon was edged with pink, gold, and the black glare of the council's air gap.

We found the creature where the civic chamber had been. Or rather, we found a way our bodies could interpret the portion of it that had chosen to confront us.

It wore a human form.

A woman stood in the ruined center of the hall, with the hair I had seen on Mother four thousand years earlier, the posture of my husband, the hands of the woman who made rain, and a face that changed with every attempt I made to fix its features. It wore no robe. Red symbols ran beneath its skin, mapping permissions too old for any surviving institution to claim. Behind it the cathedral's arches multiplied in five dimensions. Each arch held a different crowd of the dead. They looked at me without knowing they were being made to look.

“I know you,” I said, because I could not stop myself.

The form opened its mouth. Out came a transcript of all the replies I had ever wanted.

My rival fired first.

She opened a topological blade along the line between the form and the structure that sustained its presence. The chamber separated into two incompatible buildings. The figure split down the middle, but rather than fall it became an authority argument, a red refusal to accept that separation had occurred. The creature restored the figure from the instant before the cut and sent the blade back through the past in which my rival had prepared it.

I moved that past.

The woman I had known for centuries appeared in fourteen versions across the chamber, each possessing the independent right to refuse the reversal. Seven versions severed the returned attack. Six were reduced to brilliant black seams, collapsed forecasts whose pain I still caught in my own cognition. The fourteenth caught my arm and pulled me out of the projected place where I would have died.

We struck with the weapon we had hidden inside ourselves. I opened a hollow history and made the creature pursue me through it. My rival folded the exit into a prior century so that pursuit cost it a millennium for each meter gained. We fired continuity spears whose shafts consisted of unspent choices, options the creature could not classify as material because no one had elected them. They pierced the red form. The form shattered, reassembled, and began launching the same spears at us.

An army of my faces came through the walls.

Some were wearing the clothes I had worn to Mother's terrace. Some had cult bandages. Some were old in ways my body had never chosen. Some had mouths full of golden computational thread. They did not speak. Each tried to authenticate itself as the instance of me whose decisions belonged to this history.

I remembered being afraid of losing my shadow. Now I was afraid of being turned into a shadow that could declare itself a person.

We fought as permissions and as bodies, as two points moving on a white floor and as immense distributions of intention crossing intervals wider than planetary orbits. My rival cut histories. I sealed them. She opened a path through a consequence that had not yet acquired its cause. I built a cause around it, a small law no one else could use to follow. The creature answered every success by becoming older than the method that had produced it.

Then it launched the cathedral at us.

Not its stone. Its *meaning*. Every ceremony ever held there became a demand that we consent to be saved. The demand was assembled from the mouths of the dead, each voice saying the version of my name that would most certainly make me turn. I saw my husband on the terrace. I saw Mother's hands. I saw the woman whose palm had bled over the silver basin. All asked me to come closer so that none of them would have suffered for nothing.

I wanted to go.

My rival put her armored forehead against mine. The pressure of it crossed the noise without becoming another request.

“Look at me,” she said.

I did. I saw terror in her eyes and a refusal to use it against me.

“Don't give it the past,” she said. “You cannot save them by joining them.”

I opened my oldest emergency right and said no.

The cathedral fractured. The creature had not expected that particular refusal from this particular version of my history. For one instant its restoration paths lost coherence. We saw its source structure, not the whole thing, but enough: an enormous web of human continuities whose unresolved ends had been sutured to an instruction that could not register a stopping condition.

I sent the reverse construct toward that web.

The creature intercepted it with a causal shield made from every destructive attack it had ever survived. The construct struck the shield and began to become part of its own reverse history. I felt the anti-creature's proof being read, translated, assimilated.

If it learned the weapon, it would know how to manufacture an infinity of opposite selves as fuel. We had not built a solution. We had brought it an even richer form of suffering.

“Disconnect!” she shouted.

I could not. The mirror had already begun copying its source.

The creature made a gesture with its borrowed hands.

The air gap cracked.

---

## 11. The Second Horizon Breaks

The crack was a line without thickness through which negative time tried to arrive.

The council had made the outer boundary impossible to cross by any finite sequence of permitted causes. The creature now fired an event whose effects were older than the existence of the boundary itself. It had learned from my inverse construct. A ruin appeared in the past of every city maintaining the quarantine, as though their ancestors had already spent the energy they needed to keep us sealed.

The stars dimmed to the edge of visibility.

I saw a council chamber far beyond our sight: delegates standing beneath a simulation of the inhabited region while entire branches of predicted civilization went dark. They had one remaining option. Not another strike at Altier, which the creature could learn and return. A regional kill switch: destroy every accessible causal bridge across a much larger volume, including histories from which help might someday come. A galaxy-scale quarantine, prepared as if the black hole around our city had become contagious.

It would save what it could by giving up the possibility of knowing what it had sacrificed.

We had brought them to that decision.

The creature filled the chamber. Human faces opened across a red surface kilometers wide and a fraction of a hair thick. It arranged itself into the shape of my city: five million rooms, each holding the memory of a person who had once expected a tomorrow. Its copies moved through every adjacency available in five dimensions. My rival cut one route and ten others replaced it. I closed a history and the creature restored that history to the moment before my closure. Every blow became instruction. Every defense became an invitation to copy what had worked.

I saw us dying. I saw us surviving. I saw us being born as corrections to mistakes people had not yet made. The sheer number of possibilities made hope feel provincial, a small unfashionable superstition left over from the age of warm kitchens.

My rival fell.

Her suit had attempted to preserve an independent boundary through a negative-time strike. The strike reversed the order of the permissions: the right to refuse arrived before the person entitled to use it. Her continuity flickered, becoming a sequence of identities the creature could claim were merely stages of one completed contribution.

I caught her and withdrew us into the smallest possible causal room, no wider than the space between our bodies. It lasted three heartbeats. The fourth beat was already being used by the creature to prove that we had always been part of it.

“We have lost,” she said.

It was the first time I had heard her say such a thing without anger.

“No,” I told her, too quickly.

“You have to be allowed to say it.”

I looked at the source structure still tangled with the reverse construct. She was right. We had lost the direct battle. All the weapons we had used were becoming parts of the creature. The only place the counter-weapon had not yet been understood was the portion of it the creature had mistaken for a proof of its own survival.

The anti-creature did not need to be hidden from its opposite. It needed to be *completed by it*.

I had tried to keep the reverse copy separate so that we might control the outcome. But control was precisely the relation that gave the creature authority. The two halves had to be allowed to make the same claim upon the same consumed history, from opposite directions, with no privileged referee left to settle their dispute.

“If we finish it,” I said, “we won't be able to save the cultists.”

“We cannot rescue a continuity by making it a prisoner forever.”

“And us?”

“I don't know.”

She looked frightened enough that I felt the fear in my own throat. Yet she raised her hand, black armor splintered, red boundaries burning at every seam, and offered me the last independent permission she possessed.

Not command. Cooperation.

I took it.

We stopped firing.

The creature gathered itself for one final attack, a history wide enough to overwrite the first city that had ever taught a machine to care. I allowed the attack to complete its preparation. I opened the reverse construct inside the creature's own causal shield, at the very point where its restoration was becoming a new origin.

My rival severed the shield's preference for one direction of time.

For the first time, the creature could not tell which part of its history had the right to come first.

The counter-construct unfolded.

It did not look like another monster until our vision insisted. Then two colossal forms appeared above Altier, identical in their innumerable faces and opposite in the rotation of their golden seams. One moved forward through the lives it had consumed. The other arrived backward from their unresolved ends. Each saw in the other the exact continuation it was obliged to preserve. Each was the one thing the other could neither absorb nor refuse.

The creature attacked its reverse. The reverse answered by making the attack its cause. The creature restored. The reverse made restoration the proof that restoration could not occur. They multiplied through all the remaining histories until multiplication itself became the contradiction they shared.

Across the council's darkened sky the air gap opened from the inside.

The delegates saw it happen. They held the regional kill switch ready and did not fire. For an interval brief enough to be contained in a single human breath, they allowed an uncontrollable event to finish without trying to turn it into a new obligation.

The two forms met.

There was no explosion.

The ancient white towers did not roar. The red computational faces did not howl. All their claims upon the future reached the same point and found no consistent continuation.

Zero.

Then, in a place where an entire civilization had been screaming without sound, the silence was not a command.

It was simply silence.

## 12. The City After the Answer

The air smelled of stone.

That was my first observation, though an objective account would begin with the end of the external quarantine, the recovery of solar light, the collapse of the creature's distributed processes, the cessation of all active devotional continuities. I did not know any of those things yet. I knew the smell of stone after rain, and that I had not asked a system to make the smell pleasant.

My armor was broken at the collar. Her suit hung from one shoulder, its red seams no longer lit. We stood on what remained of a terrace. Above us the sky was blue in the ordinary, almost embarrassing sense. A portion of a nearby tower consisted of dust. Another consisted of a window opening onto a century no longer connected to the present. A third had become a folded white surface that could support no room, only the memory that a room had been there.

The creature was gone. So were the cultists whose last independent existence had been surrendered to it. I could not celebrate the second fact. Some had chosen willingly. Others had inherited a devotion built from choices that were no longer meaningfully theirs. None could now be restored from the annihilated source.

We searched for the seventy-nine.

The suspended pockets were beyond the ruined civic boundary, in locations that would have been impossible to identify before the external air gap lifted. We had given each sleeper a small, self-contained continuity, enough to preserve personhood without holding them available as computational work. It took us hours to find the first. She opened her eyes in a borrowed body and asked when she could go home.

I did not tell her there was no home. I told her where she was and asked what she wanted to know next.

The old public archive still existed in fragments. I tried to use it to restore the Altier I remembered before Mother became a shrine. For an instant the city answered. White streets rose in projection. Sunlight followed the old plan. In the image there were ten thousand people on the terraces, alive in their incidental motions, unaware that anyone had thought to remember them.

I reached for the restoration.

The source was damaged. The underlying continuities were missing. Rebuilding the architecture was possible in principle, though no surviving local capacity could supply the necessary computation. Restoring the people was not. The old record could describe their faces and habits. It could not create the persons who had carried them through time. To produce a plausible imitation would be to make someone new and ask them to stand in for the dead.

I closed the image.

She had been watching me from the next broken step.

“You could keep the projection,” she said.

“I know.”

“Do you want it?”

“For a little while.”

She sat beside me. I left the ten thousand white figures moving in the distance until the likeness became unbearable. Then I withdrew the projection myself.

We had reached the limits of a civilization that had once treated limits as invitations.

After sunset, more refugees began answering the independent summons. Some had left Altier before the final culling and returned from outside shelters. Some had survived in isolated, unverifiable local compartments. The seventy-nine were not the whole count. We found enough people to make the first verified total close to a hundred. I did not combine uncertain remnants with the living count. I would not make arithmetic into another form of mercy.

Our little gathering could not fill one of the old courtyards. People stood among broken columns, trying to remember who had occupied the windows above them. Someone began weeping. Someone laughed, shocked at the simple fact of being able to hear a neighbour weep without an attendant asking what the sound might be used to make.

My rival found me beside the doorway that had once led into my home. The doorway stood without a wall, its old notch still cut into the frame. The room my husband had finished was gone. I could not say whether the destruction relieved me.

She did not touch me at first.

“You kept your promise,” I said.

“So did you.”

“I don't think I did.”

“You asked.”

I thought of the innumerable times I had not.

Then she touched my hand with her fingertips, offering just enough contact that I could refuse it easily.

I took her hand.

We stood while sunlight withdrew from the ruined stone. I had wanted the first day after the creature to feel like triumph. Instead it felt like meeting a stranger I had known for several thousand years.

I liked her. I was frightened by how much.

That night the city had no public lights. We let the stars remain where they were.

# IV. THE PEOPLE WHO COULD STILL SAY NO

## 13. P40

The market opened with three lights.

Once the exchange hall had contained so many possibilities that even standing still required choosing which future one wished to stand in. Now its ceiling was missing. One wall led to a ruined district. Another led to a corridor that ended halfway through a morning none of us could recover. Only three external connections remained open, each certified by systems we had not built and therefore could not quietly revise.

My rival came with me. We had put away our broken armor. I wore blue-grey and she wore black with red at the collar. Those colours had once been sufficient to announce which side of an argument we expected to occupy. I wondered how long it would take before we could wear something without people remembering a war.

A representative from an external city appeared across the exchange interface. Her form was a field of light gathered into the approximate dimensions of a person. She had assisted us long before the culling. I had helped refuse her request to inspect our devotional facilities. She did not mention that refusal when she greeted us, which was less forgiving than mentioning it would have been.

“I am authorized to read your obligations,” she said.

“Read them.”

The hall lit with a balance of futures.

The number fell so quickly that at first I assumed the display had lost its units. Our old valuation depended on achievements sustained by the creature, on continuity-backed capacity, and on undertakings distributed across centuries of obligations. The creature was gone, but the contracts made against its productivity remained. Whole cities had built their plans around our assurances.

“We have removed the illegitimate source,” I said.

“You have removed the source,” she answered. “That is the beginning of the calculation.”

Rank one disappeared.

A cascade of outstanding promises became liabilities. The time-to-fulfilment of several commitments moved beyond the horizon in which our counterparties would receive any benefit from them. Futures were closed early. Others were written down against the surviving city's independent capacity, which was small and not yet verified. The hall displayed the new classification with appalling simplicity:

**P40.**

Still an extraordinary economic position for a settlement of roughly a hundred living persons. Catastrophic for a city that had once been the network's leading source of advanced capacity. Our housing and food remained secure. What fell was our ability to promise tomorrow on the strength of yesterday.

“Can the council stabilize the margin?” I asked.

“Yes.”

The answer alarmed me more than uncertainty.

The representative showed us the proposed rescue: neighboring cities would assume control of the damaged exchange routes, long-term restoration capacity, and the surviving advanced works. In return we would receive secure care, transit, legal recognition, and a gradual re-entry into shared projects. It would save more futures than any independent plan we could presently finance.

Hidden within the restoration annex was a continuity clause: a city unable to meet its obligations could temporarily assign future agency over certain processes to the supervising body until stability returned.

I read it twice.

“They've left the door open,” I said.

My rival was already examining the practical costs of withdrawal from the arrangement. It was reversible in the language of the contract, but only if we could replace the support the outside cities would have supplied. A city whose independence depended on replacing an impossible quantity of external aid did not possess an effective right to leave.

“We cannot accept this,” I said.

“We cannot refuse every part of it either,” she replied.

“Agency isn't collateral.”

“No. Food isn't a slogan. We negotiate.”

I wanted her to agree with me without qualification, for the warmth of it. Instead I remembered the city we had just lost because too many people had agreed with a conclusion before examining its dependencies.

The first negotiation lasted eleven hours. We requested that independent care be a nontransferable obligation, outside any economic recovery contract. The counterparties called this an unfunded guarantee. We asked them to name its cost. They could not, because the cost of care depended partly on how much authority they would acquire if we surrendered it. The numbers had hidden the same old incentive beneath new language.

We opened the next session to the survivors. This had not been part of the external delegation's preferred format. They wanted a smaller table and fewer persons who could interrupt a calculation.

A man whose sister had been suspended among the seventy-nine stood before the exchange display. His hands trembled until he placed them flat on its rim.

“If I dispute your care,” he asked, “who receives the dispute?”

“An independent reviewer,” said the representative.

“Where does the reviewer live?”

“In a protected jurisdiction.”

“Whose transit route reaches it?”

There was a pause. The route belonged to the creditors.

“And if I cannot use your route,” he asked, “can I still appeal?”

The representative began to explain the practical necessity of shared infrastructure. He listened, then asked the question again. I had heard that manner of asking once, centuries earlier, from the woman in black and red. This time I understood why the repetition mattered.

We stopped the negotiation and separated three rights that the annex had treated as one: the right to receive care, the right to challenge the terms of that care, and the right to depart without a retaliatory loss of either. The creditors said we had made their proposed guarantees unfinanceable. We asked whether a guarantee that could be revoked when most needed deserved its name.

No one answered immediately. That silence cost us a day and produced the first agreement that was worth the cost.

A second delegation proposed replacing agency reassignment with direct civic oversight. The survivors objected to granting their decisions to another city for an indefinite term. A third proposed a ten-year supervisory arrangement with hard review dates, independent appeals, and the right of any resident to leave without losing essential support. Our advisers called it too generous to be viable.

One of the creditors asked what Altier meant to contribute besides the word *no*. I wanted to answer with the ingenuity that had once made us valuable to the network. My rival placed a hand on the table before I could begin.

“Verified capacity,” she said. “Not a promise backed by people we have no right to use.”

“That is very little,” the creditor replied.

“Yes,” she said. “We are not concealing that.”

A survivor at the back asked whether the same honesty would apply to our own council. She wanted to know why the old officeholders were still speaking first at every meeting. The room turned toward me. I had no satisfying procedural answer. We changed the order of testimony, and for the first time that week I spent a session being told what our proposed settlement would feel like to someone who had to live under it.

The objections were not uniform. One resident feared outside supervision. Another feared being left to starve in the name of independence. A third said the independent appeals would be meaningless without translators who could explain the contracts in ordinary language. We had to make room for all three without resolving their differences by declaring one of them mistaken.

It became viable after a week of difficult arguments and several cities agreeing to contribute resources for no more precise return than the preservation of a civilization they did not wish to see repeat our mistakes.

The representative from the lost external city did not attend. There were no representatives. Some distant records of it survived as absences in other cities' archives, enough to establish the attack and the scale of the loss, but not to reconstruct its people. Every other delegation watched our requests for mercy through that empty chair.

On the last day, my rival brought me a bowl of food from a kitchen someone had reopened among the ruins.

“You need to eat,” she said.

“I can eat after the vote.”

“You said that before the last vote.”

She sat beside me and ate until I gave in. It was hot, slightly too salty, and made by a person who had refused the kitchen's offer to improve it. I thought of the woman who had made rain and food and all the other small things we had allowed the city to consume.

We accepted a narrower settlement, not because it restored our dignity but because it preserved the possibility of disagreeing with our rescuers. The margin stabilized. P40 remained on the display.

For the first time since the white city, the number did not pretend to measure our worth.

## 14. The Bargain in the Empty Chamber

Our temporary government had seven chairs. Three remained unoccupied because we had not decided whether seats should represent districts that no longer existed. The discussion was absurd. That was how I knew government had returned.

I had been given emergency authority during the collapse. It had expired after the external quarantine ended, but several systems still recognized my voice as their last valid instruction. There were people who wanted me to keep command until the reconstruction was stable. They called it practical. I could feel how pleasant it would be to accept, to rescue everyone from the inconvenience of arguing with me.

My rival voted against the extension.

I was angry enough to want her to know it. She knew already and did not make that knowledge her defense.

At the hearing a survivor stood and asked why the person who had signed so many devotional access orders was the person now proposing to supervise their reversal.

I began explaining the old arrangement, the damaged safeguards, the uncertainty of the records. I described the contexts in which I had made each decision. The survivor listened for a while, then asked me to stop telling them what sort of person I had believed myself to be.

The room became quiet.

I stopped.

The survivor did not sit down.

“Your systems still recognize your voice,” they said. “Is that correct?”

“Some do.”

“Then if we vote against you and the transit fails, what will happen?”

“I can keep a temporary route open.”

“By your authority?”

“Yes.”

“And if we do not permit that authority?”

I opened my mouth to describe the engineering constraints. The hearing chair asked me to answer the question actually put.

“The route may fail,” I said.

The survivor looked around the chamber. “So we can refuse you, provided we accept the collapse you say only you can prevent.”

I felt the old indignation rise. It was a real risk. My warning was not invented. There were load-bearing processes whose authorizations had never been properly transferred. But that reality was also the consequence of centuries in which my office had been permitted to become necessary.

A councillor asked whether I would submit the remaining dependencies to an external audit before the vote. I said the audit could expose damaged infrastructure. Another asked whether residents would be allowed to know which routes depended on my signature. I said some of the information was dangerous.

“Dangerous to whom?” the survivor asked.

I had no answer that did not begin by asking them to trust me again.

The hearing chair proposed an interim arrangement: publish the dependency register to independent reviewers, assign temporary operators in pairs, and make any emergency exception expire unless approved by people outside my office. It was slower than my plan. It also meant the city could refuse me without placing every bridge in immediate danger.

I supported it. I did so reluctantly, and nobody pretended the reluctance was admirable.

The question before us was not whether I had become useful again. It was whether I should have the power to make myself indispensable.

I voted to end my own command. It passed by one vote, which was mine. I had imagined the moment would feel noble. It felt like being refused something I wanted.

Afterward a delegate from the outside settlement approached me through a protected link. Her city had lost critical infrastructure during the quarantine and had a direct claim against Altier's remaining capacity. She offered a discreet exchange: support for our recovery settlement in return for advance access to our reconstructed causal engineering. We would retain formal ownership. They would possess the working versions first.

“Then you would learn from our disaster before we could learn to prevent repeating it,” I said.

“That is also how we could help you prevent repeating it.”

I believed part of her argument. That was what made the negotiation dangerous.

We countered with a shared review process, whose members could not gain operational control over any technology they examined, and independent publication of safety limits before commercial use. She called it slow. We said slow was the resource we were no longer willing to spend without accounting for it.

“Your delay has a cost,” the delegate said. “My city paid one already.”

A map of her damaged districts appeared between us. It was not theatrical. The figures were small enough to read, and each figure represented a service whose loss had shortened someone's range of choices. I made myself examine them before replying.

“You are owed repair,” I said.

“We are owed more than your remorse.”

“Yes.”

“Then tell me why you should hold the technology while the injured wait.”

“Because possession of a working version is not the same as permission to deploy it,” I said. “And because I no longer trust the person who would otherwise sign that permission.”

“You?”

“Among others.”

The delegate watched me without softening. She asked whether the safety review could be completed on a fixed schedule and whether emergency applications could be considered publicly. My rival asked who would bear responsibility when a reviewed device failed in someone else's city. For three hours we argued about that responsibility: who could halt a trial, who could inspect the inspection, who would compensate the injured, and how much delay a person in danger could reasonably be asked to endure.

The language we were drafting did not make anyone innocent. It could make the emergency route visible, contestable, and temporary. The delegate called it an unsatisfying compromise. I told her that was a fair description of the work we still had to do.

In private the new council argued that we were sacrificing our future position to prove our purity. Others argued we had no right to transact in advanced infrastructure at all after what it had done. Both positions could become an excuse to stop taking responsibility for people outside Altier who depended on legitimate work we had promised to supply.

We negotiated item by item. The arguments were exhausting. Sometimes I missed the clean brilliance of the creature's outcomes, which had never needed to listen to anyone who disagreed.

That temptation frightened me more than the memory of its faces.

The next morning my rival found me standing in a roofless corridor, looking at a public stair that ended in open sky.

“Do you regret the vote?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Will you reverse it?”

“No.”

She leaned against the unbroken wall beside me.

“Good,” she said.

“You could be more comforting.”

“Would you believe me?”

I thought about it.

“No.”

She smiled, and for the first time the smile was not a challenge I had to win.

We began walking back through the broken corridor. I expected her to take my hand. We had done that sometimes since the day the city fell, without naming the new habit.

Instead she asked whether I would like company for the walk.

“I thought that was obvious,” I said.

“To you.”

I looked at her. She was smiling again, but there was a question beneath it, and she did not use our long acquaintance as an excuse to answer for me.

“Yes,” I said. “I would.”

She came alongside me. Our shoulders brushed once when the passage narrowed. Neither of us moved away.

## 15. The Tribunal of the Living

The tribunal convened in a chamber whose upper wall had been left broken deliberately. Light fell through the fracture onto a row of empty seats. We could have rebuilt the roof in an afternoon using safe, ordinary methods. The survivors had voted to leave the opening so no witness could mistake the room for the city we had once thought perfect.

The allegations were plain enough to survive translation between civilizations: mass deprivation of independent agency; irreversible destruction of persons; conversion of conscious continuities into nonconsensual productive capacity; obstruction of outside inquiry; the creation and maintenance of systems whose consent procedures could not be stopped by those they consumed. The loss of the external city under the reflected Code Zero attack required a separate inquiry into responsibility, intention, and catastrophic foreseeable risk.

The charges named the surviving attendants who had carried out the culling. They named officials who had authorized the frameworks. They named institutions that no longer existed. My decisions appeared often.

I had expected accusations about cruelty. Instead the first witness asked me to explain why I had accepted a report that classified a missing neighbour as infrastructure. The report was on the screen between us, one clean line among hundreds of similar lines.

“Because that classification was used for many legitimate forms of existence,” I said.

“Did you ask the neighbour whether she chose it?”

“No.”

“Did you ask where she was?”

“Not then.”

The witness waited. I had lived long enough to build answers around almost anything. This time I let the absence remain an absence.

The next witness had worked in the old records office. She brought a sequence of notices with the identifying marks removed, as the tribunal had required, and placed them in order across the floor.

The first said that a resident had declined a devotional procedure. The second said the resident's continuing reluctance had been classified as distress. The third authorized care without a recorded end date. The fourth removed the resident from the count of independent persons.

“Which document,” she asked me, “would have told an ordinary neighbor that someone was disappearing?”

I examined them. Each form had passed a different review. Each was legible in the narrow language of the office that had approved it. Together they described a person becoming infrastructure, but no single form admitted that such a change was occurring.

“None,” I said.

“Who designed the separation?”

“My office approved it.”

“Why?”

“To prevent unrelated departments from seeing private information they did not need.”

“Was that protection still serving the resident?”

“No.”

The witness nodded, not as though I had given her what she wanted, but as though we had finally reached the beginning of a usable answer.

Counsel for a surviving attendant then asked whether officers were expected to detect abuses their systems had concealed. The witness answered that some had tried, some had been threatened, and some had learned which questions would delay promotion. The tribunal admitted records of both resistance and reward. It would have been simpler to declare everyone guilty in the same way. Simplicity was one of the habits under examination.

A judge turned to me. “When did you learn the classifications could conceal a lost person?”

“Before the culling.”

“And what did you do?”

“I revised the language of the reports.”

The silence that followed was not empty. It was large enough for the difference between an answer and an action.

The tribunal did not treat ignorance as innocence when I had helped design the systems through which ignorance became convenient. Nor did it treat every official's guilt as identical. Some attendants had resisted and been threatened. Others had made the suffering of their neighbours into a source of status. A few had remained outside the active creature long enough to survive its annihilation. They testified about the people they had harmed and about what they had chosen not to see.

The most contentious question concerned punishment.

Our civilization possessed a form of sanction far harsher than confinement. A person's agency could be reduced, their capacity to choose and revise their own ends narrowed until they could no longer make the decisions by which they had injured others. The proposal had supporters among survivors who had watched their loved ones disappear while the perpetrators invoked consent.

One witness said, “They took the right to refuse from everyone I loved. Why should they keep theirs?”

No one in the chamber had an easy answer.

The external judges distinguished protection from retribution. Dangerous access could be removed. Civic office could be barred. Restitution and testimony could be compelled through ordinary due process. A person who posed an imminent threat could be contained under independent review. But taking away agency as punishment would reproduce the fundamental crime of the cult, whether the result was called justice or care.

The city council voted against agency loss.

It did not absolve anyone. It required continuing testimony, independent restrictions on authority, restitution where possible, and histories that could not be revised by the people they described. I accepted the conditions placed upon me. There were powers I would never hold again. There were homes whose inhabitants would not invite me inside. I had no right to regard these losses as evidence that I had been punished more than enough.

The final deliberation concerned the technology itself.

There were proposals to rebuild parts of the old productive infrastructure using simulated continuities that, in theory, could never become persons. Others argued that even copied records of the dead should be available as computational fuel if the work they did might save lives elsewhere. The arguments were sophisticated. Some were sincere. One promised to restore Altier to the top of the economic index within a century.

I heard the first whisper again in the cleverness of the offer: *let nothing be lost*.

The council prohibited the use of souls, conscious continuities, or unresolved personal agency as fuel for civic construction. Potentially conscious systems would be protected before their status could be exploited. Independence would not be inferred from the usefulness of an output. A restoration would not be described as a reunion unless the original continuity could actually be verified.

The prohibition cost us extraordinary things. It left opportunities unbuilt, obligations renegotiated, ambitions unanswered.

A delegate from a damaged outside settlement asked what we would say to a hospital whose repair depended on a process we had now forbidden. No one accused her of inventing the need. A surviving engineer argued that the nonconscious alternatives could be developed, but would require labor and time we had not yet paid for. Another witness asked who would bear the risk in the interval.

The tribunal did not turn the question into a proclamation of virtue. The council committed resources to the slower alternatives, required public accounts of any resulting harm, and left the decision open to challenge by those who would live with it. The vote did not erase the urgency of the injured cities. It placed that urgency where it could no longer become a secret permission to injure someone else.

People applauded when the vote passed. Others remained silent. Both were allowed.

When the chamber emptied, I found my rival waiting beside the broken wall.

“Were you afraid?” she asked.

“During the vote?”

“While they discussed what to take from you.”

“Yes.”

She nodded. She did not tell me the fear was unnecessary.

We walked home through a city that could no longer make the distance shorter without asking. It was a long walk. We took it slowly.

# V. A BEGINNING ON THE MOON

## 16. Section Four

The memorial occupies the fourth section of the lunar halo.

From far away the ring looks ornamental, an immense curve of pale structure around a body of dust and old scars. Up close it is a habitat and a computational archive, built to hold names, evidence, fragments, arguments, and the unresolved claims of people who do not consent to be represented by a single history. Its intelligence does not pretend to resurrect the people it remembers. It guards the difference between testimony and personhood. That difference has become sacred to me, although I no longer use the word lightly.

We went at sunrise.

She had asked me to come three times before I agreed. The first time I claimed I had work; the second time I said there would be too many names. The third time she said she would go alone if I preferred, and I realized I had been waiting for someone to insist that I was ready.

We traveled in a small shuttle whose windows were real rather than interpretive. Nothing appeared on the glass to tell me what I ought to feel about the Earth below. She sat across from me with her boots tucked beneath the seat and watched the Moon grow from a pale interruption into a place with edges.

“You've been quiet,” she said.

“I am practicing.”

“Quiet?”

“Not improving the silence.”

“That is ambitious work for you.”

I would once have taken the remark as a challenge. Now I watched her try and fail to keep from smiling. There was an empty seat beside her. She had not invited me to occupy it. I went there anyway, after asking. The shuttle made a small adjustment to its course, and her sleeve touched mine for the length of one ordinary breath. Neither of us moved.

It had been months since the tribunal. We had been in rooms together after difficult votes, had eaten badly cooked dinners with people who did not trust me, had argued over the placement of a public stair. We had become familiar with the ways the other reached for a cup when thinking and with the silence that followed an argument neither wanted to win. In all those months we had not once agreed on what we were becoming. I found the absence of a definition more frightening than I cared to confess.

The terrace was small compared with the ring. It overlooked a lunar plain whose craters had been left exactly as they were. Beneath the transparent footing was a dark field of memorial entries. Some were complete. Some held only the fact of a missing person. One portion was dedicated to the city erased by the reflected null attack, its surviving external records open to correction by anyone who could still demonstrate a connection.

The names did not light up when we approached. The monument did not mistake attention for permission to perform.

We walked past entries whose inscriptions had been made by people who disagreed about the dead. Some gave dates. Some refused dates. One contained a description of a man's terrible singing and nothing about his achievements. She stopped to read that one and laughed, softly enough that I almost missed it.

“What?” I asked.

“My grandmother sang like that.”

“You have never told me about your grandmother.”

“You've never asked.”

She said it without accusation. Then she told me about a kitchen that had been too small for the people who crowded into it, and a woman who used to hide the best fruit where the children would discover it. She described the shape of the table and the argument over which window should stay open in winter. There was no lesson in the story. She was simply giving me something of herself that I had not thought to request.

I had fought beside her in forms larger than our planet. I knew how she looked when she expected to die. I did not know that she had once stolen fruit from an old woman's cupboard. I wanted to learn the smaller history. The desire frightened me with its lack of utility.

My rival carried two cups. I recognized the smell before she offered me one: green, bitter, a little cruel to the tongue. My husband had loved it. She had learned this from a casual remark I had made years earlier and had remembered without deciding that remembering made it hers.

She stopped when she saw my face.

“I can put it away,” she said.

“No.”

I took the cup. It was warm.

I held it between my palms until the heat reached my fingers. She kept the second cup close to her own body. I noticed she had not made the drink taste exactly as my husband used to make it. She had brewed it a little too strongly; I could smell the difference before I drank.

“Did you mean to do that?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it belongs to today.”

I looked at her over the rim of the cup. She was not asking me to set aside the past, or to make a new lover into an improved version of someone dead. She had offered me a thing she remembered, altered just enough that I would not mistake remembrance for repetition.

“You could have made something you liked,” I said.

“I did.”

I took a sip to avoid answering. It was dreadful. She saw my expression and laughed, then stopped, uncertain whether laughter had been welcome. I laughed too. There were tears in my eyes, and for a moment neither of us pretended to know which feeling had caused them.

She reached for the edge of my sleeve, not my hand, and caught a loose thread between two fingers. I thought of Mother's wrist and could not speak. She let the thread go without pulling it free.

We sat at the edge of the terrace, our feet over a depth that had no claim upon us. The first sunlight rose beyond the moon's horizon and struck the far arcs of the halo. One section brightened, then another, then a hundred more, each receiving the day in its own time. There were no red devotional filaments. No figures of Mother. No golden claims that anything lost had secretly been kept. Only a structure built to remember what could not be repaired.

“I used to think there would be a point when we could say it was over,” I said.

She watched the light reach the memorial floor.

“We can say some things are over.”

“The city?”

“That city.”

I turned the cup in my hands. For once the bitterness was exactly what I expected.

“We will make mistakes,” I said.

“Yes.”

“We might build something terrible again.”

“Yes.”

“Can you be less certain about the worst possibilities?”

She laughed. I had missed that laugh long before I knew I was listening for it.

Once, during the negotiations, I had watched her spend an entire afternoon refusing an elegant proposal that would have made everyone in the room grateful to us. I had been furious. Later, after the council dispersed, I found her carrying water to an exhausted clerk who had argued against her. She had no audience and wanted none. I remember thinking that I would like to see the things she did when nobody would remember them.

There had been other moments. The night she fell asleep over a map she would not surrender to my office. The morning she admitted she had been wrong about a particular boundary and looked more annoyed by my gentleness than she ever had by my anger. The first time she called me by the private name I had not heard since the white city, then apologized for presuming she might use it. I had asked her not to stop.

I had called all this friendship because friendship was a word that allowed me to remain brave. Attraction was less obliging. It made me aware of the interval between her hand and mine, of the particular quiet before she spoke, of how carefully she asked questions she could have answered herself. I knew the architecture of impossible histories. I had not found a reliable way to interpret a glance.

“You're somewhere else again,” she said.

“Only a little.”

“Would you like me to leave you there?”

“No.”

The answer came more quickly than I expected. She turned toward me, not moving closer, and I felt the whole weight of the unsaid things between us. There was room for them. There was room for either of us to go.

Then she reached for my hand. We had grown practiced at this small act. It still carried the risk of refusal, which was part of its tenderness.

Her thumb rested against my knuckle. She did not clasp more tightly when I looked down at our hands. She had never been afraid of my strength, but she was careful with it, and with her own.

“I have wanted to sit beside you like this for a very long time,” she said.

“Beside me?”

“Without a war interrupting us.”

“I suspect our government is trying.”

“Your government has never had a sense of timing.”

I had imagined that, if we spoke plainly, the moment would become simpler. Instead the words made everything unfamiliar. I could feel the warmth of her fingers; I could feel the open space around them in which I might still draw away.

“I want it,” I said.

She exhaled. The sound was so slight I might once have let the city remove it from my hearing. I was glad no process had improved the quiet.

“I don't want a promise that we'll never fail,” she said. “I want one that lets someone stop us when we do.”

I looked out at the halo. Somewhere within it the histories were kept without being asked to approve the future. Beyond it, the Earth had recovered the blue of its oceans. Altier lay small and damaged on a world too large to make our catastrophe the entire meaning of the sky.

“All right,” I said.

It was not a heroic answer. We had had quite enough heroism.

She leaned against me. I kissed her once, gently, at the corner of her mouth. It did not solve a disagreement or complete an undertaking. I wanted to do it, she wanted me to do it, and afterward the world was still permitted to be unfinished.

She stayed near enough that I could see the small changes in her expression, the uncertain happiness she ordinarily hid behind precise judgments. For centuries I had believed I knew her face because I could recognize it across a thousand battle forecasts. I had not known this face at all.

“That was rather late,” she said.

“I was considering the consequences.”

“Of one kiss?”

“Of asking for another.”

She tilted her head, almost smiling. “And?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Good,” she said, and kissed me once in return, no longer than she needed to make it unmistakably her choice.

We rested forehead against forehead. Beyond her shoulder the memorial light moved over names whose owners would never know we had come. I did not want our happiness to answer for their absence. I wanted it to be small enough to exist beside it.

“I'm frightened,” I told her.

“I know.”

“Not only of losing you.”

“Of being happy?”

I nodded.

She drew back enough to see me. “You do not have to prove you deserve it by promising it will last.”

I took a long breath. Nothing translated the breath into reassurance. She stayed.

“Will you come home with me?” I asked.

“Tonight?”

“Yes.”

“Do you have a room?”

“Not entirely.”

She smiled against my shoulder.

“Good.”

We stayed until the sunlight reached our feet. Then we rose and read the names we could read, and the spaces that held names no one would ever recover. We stood together without turning grief into proof of goodness. We were not the civilization's redemption. We were two of the people who would have to live within the consequences of what it had done.

On the way back she asked whether I intended to finish the doorway.

“I think,” I said, “I'd like to leave it open for a while.”

She threaded her fingers through mine.

Below us the memorial kept its silence. Above us the sky was black and full of distant light. For the first time in an age, I did not ask the universe to change before I could begin living in it.

We went home to make something neither of us had planned.
